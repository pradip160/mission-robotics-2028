## 2. Acceleration, gravity, and physical meaning (continuation after Day 72)

I continued from the Day 72 foot-height model:

$$
z(t)=H\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right).
$$

Differentiating position with respect to time gives vertical velocity:

$$
\dot z(t)=H\frac{\pi}{T}\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right).
$$

Differentiating velocity gives vertical acceleration:

$$
\ddot z(t)=-H\left(\frac{\pi}{T}\right)^2\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right).
$$

For $H=0.20$ m and $T=4$ s:

$$
z(t)=0.20\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right),\quad
\dot z(t)=0.20\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right),\quad
\ddot z(t)=-0.20\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^2\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right).
$$

**What I learned:**

- Position describes where the foot is, velocity describes the direction and rate of motion, and acceleration describes how velocity changes.
- At the highest point of this sine trajectory, vertical velocity is zero but vertical acceleration is negative. **Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration.**
- A foot may be rising while its vertical acceleration is downward: it is still moving up, but slowing down.
- We also reviewed basic Newtonian motion, gravity and $F=ma$ to connect the mathematical idea of acceleration to physical movement. A prescribed foot trajectory's acceleration is generated by a controlled leg system; it should not be confused with the constant acceleration of an object in free fall.
- The simple sine model is useful for learning but has nonzero vertical speed at takeoff and landing, so it is **not yet a validated soft-contact trajectory**.

I practised differentiation, signs, units, and sketch-based interpretation, and received corrections when I confused height, velocity, or acceleration labels.

## 3. Case Study 01 — Is a planned swing-foot trajectory safe over an obstacle?

### Problem definition

A simplified robot foot travels forward using:

$$
x(t)=0.10t,\qquad z(t)=0.20\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right), \qquad 0\leq t\leq4\ \mathrm{s}.
$$

The obstacle is located at $x=0.30$ m, has measured height $0.15$ m, and requires vertical clearance of at least $0.02$ m. Initially, errors and physical geometry were ignored to examine nominal mathematical clearance.

At the obstacle location:

$$
0.10t=0.30\ \Rightarrow\ t=3\ \mathrm{s}.
$$

The original foot height and vertical motion at this instant were:

$$
z(3)=0.20\sin(3\pi/4)\approx0.14142\ \mathrm{m},
$$

$$
\dot z(3)\approx-0.11107\ \mathrm{m/s},\qquad
\ddot z(3)\approx-0.08724\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$

The foot was descending when it reached the obstacle. The original planned height ($0.14142$ m) was even lower than the nominal obstacle height ($0.15$ m), so the model failed the clearance check.

I also compared the motion at $t=1$ s and $t=3$ s. The sine curve gives equal heights at these times, while vertical velocities have opposite signs (rising versus descending). The vertical accelerations at those times are equal and negative.

### First proposed redesign: increase peak height

I investigated raising the sine trajectory's peak from $H=0.20$ m to $H=0.25$ m, keeping the same 4-second step and horizontal motion:

$$
z_{0.25}(3)=0.25\sin(3\pi/4)\approx0.17678\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

Nominal clearance:

$$
C_{\mathrm{nominal}}=0.17678-0.15=0.02678\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

This exceeds the $0.02$ m requirement by about **$0.00678$ m (6.78 mm)** *under the nominal simplified assumptions*.

**Corrections from my handwritten work:** A few comparisons and labels were written incorrectly, including reversing an inequality sign and referring to a height as a velocity. The underlying reasoning improved after review. Raising the foot height can also increase vertical speed/acceleration, so a higher path is not automatically physically feasible.

## 4. Case Study 02 — Worst-case sensor and tracking uncertainty

We added two explicit bounded errors:

- The sensor could **underestimate** obstacle height by as much as $0.01$ m.
- The controlled foot could travel as much as $0.01$ m **lower** than planned.

For the $H=0.25$ m redesign at $t=3$ s:

$$
H_{\mathrm{obstacle,max}}=0.15+0.01=0.16\ \mathrm{m},
$$

$$
z_{\mathrm{foot,min}}=0.17678-0.01=0.16678\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

Worst-case vertical clearance:

$$
C_{\mathrm{worst}}=0.16678-0.16=0.00678\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

**Decision:** The foot would remain above the obstacle in this idealised single-point geometry, but the model **fails the specified 0.02 m clearance requirement**. A positive clearance and a satisfactory safety margin are not the same thing.

I independently identified the maximum possible obstacle height and minimum possible foot height. I initially reversed the subtraction order for clearance, which gave a negative answer; after correction, the worst-case clearance was 6.78 mm. My rejection of the trajectory was correct, but the distinction between a collision and failure of a required margin needed to be made more precise.

I considered improving sensor accuracy, improving trajectory tracking, and changing the trajectory. We verified numerically that improving both errors to $0.005$ m would still leave only $0.01678$ m worst-case clearance, short of $0.02$ m. **Component improvements must be checked against the actual requirement.**

## 5. Case Study 02B — Solve for the minimum peak height instead of guessing

The required **planned height at the obstacle** is:

$$
z_{\mathrm{required\ at\ obstacle}}
=0.15+0.01+0.01+0.02
=0.19\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

Our trajectory parameter $H$ is the *peak height*, achieved at $t=2$ s, not the foot's height at the obstacle ($t=3$ s). Therefore:

$$
H\sin(3\pi/4)\geq0.19,
$$

$$
H\geq\frac{0.19}{0.70710678}\approx0.2687\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

**Main conceptual correction:** I initially treated the required $0.19$ m crossing height as though it were $H$, then substituted the earlier ideal-condition target $0.17$ m while solving the inequality. The approach of constructing an inequality was right, but the meaning of the variables and safety requirement were mixed up. I now need to consistently distinguish **peak height** from **height at a specified time**.

### Check of a proposed $H=0.28$ m trajectory

$$
z_{0.28}(3)=0.28\sin(3\pi/4)\approx0.19799\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

$$
C_{\mathrm{worst}}=0.19799-0.01-0.16=0.02799\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

The worst-case clearance exceeds the requirement by:

$$
0.02799-0.02=0.00799\ \mathrm{m}\approx8\ \mathrm{mm}.
$$

**Decision:** $H=0.28$ m passes our simplified *bounded-error clearance test*. It is **not approved for a real humanoid** without reachability, foot geometry, contact, actuation, and balance checks.

**Arithmetic lesson:** My handwritten multiplication of $0.28\times0.7071$ was incorrect, leading me to reject this redesign. I learned to sanity-check numerical results with an approximate multiplication (for example, $0.28\times0.7=0.196$), and to verify units and comparison directions before making a safety decision.

## 6. Physical feasibility — FK, IK, joint limits, balance

I realised that passing a foot-clearance test does not establish whether the robot can actually execute the path. We connected the topics as follows:

1. **Trajectory planning:** Specifies desired foot positions $x(t),z(t)$.
2. **Coordinate transformation:** The trajectory is specified relative to the ground; our leg kinematics is relative to the hip. The controller needs the target expressed in the hip frame.
3. **Inverse kinematics (IK):** Determines candidate hip/knee angles required to reach each foot position. It may have no solution or multiple solutions.
4. **Joint limits:** Even a mathematical IK solution may violate allowed hip/knee angles.
5. **Forward kinematics (FK):** Checks where candidate joint angles actually place the foot.
6. **Velocity, dynamics and actuator limits:** A reachable path may still demand excessive joint speeds, accelerations, or motor torques.
7. **Balance and contact:** Swing-leg movement shifts mass and affects the supporting foot; even individually reachable poses may not constitute a stable walking motion.

### Reachability questions completed

For our two-link leg ($L_1=L_2=0.40$ m), maximum straight-line reach from the hip is $0.80$ m. I independently explained why a target **$0.90$ m directly below a fixed hip** is unreachable: no knee-angle change can make the two segments longer than their combined length.

### Reachability check introduced but not completed independently

A later exercise used fixed hip $P_h=(0,0.80)$ m and final foot $P_f=(0.40,0)$ m:

$$
d=\sqrt{(0.40-0)^2+(0-0.80)^2}
=\sqrt{0.80}\approx0.8944\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

This exceeds the $0.80$ m maximum reach; the fixed-hip final pose is geometrically unreachable. Moving the hip or redesigning the step could change that. **This numerical conclusion is recorded as a follow-up reference, not as a problem I independently solved in the notebook.** A whole-trajectory IK and balance verification remains unfinished.
